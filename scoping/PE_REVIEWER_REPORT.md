# Review: "A Log-Normal Model of Server CPU Utilization Under Transaction Load: Fit, Scaling, and SLA-Aware Sizing"

Journal: Performance Evaluation (Elsevier). Reviewer report for the editor.

## 1. Summary of the paper

The paper revisits a 2011 industrial claim that CPU utilization at fixed transaction load is log-normally distributed, with log-mean linear in load and a variance law derived from a multiplicative-overhead mechanism combined with Poisson arrivals. The authors fit per-VM, per-hour-of-day distributions on the two public Bitbrains GWA-T-12 traces (fastStorage and Rnd) and report that log-normal wins the AIC comparison against normal, gamma, and Weibull in 68% of 12,479 bins, with Anderson-Darling and Cramer-von Mises medians also favoring log-normal. Because Bitbrains has no transaction counts, network-received throughput serves as a load proxy; the mean clause of the scaling law is directionally confirmed, and the sigma clause grows with load but roughly nine times faster than the derived form predicts, which the authors attribute to load-proportional demand fluctuation and fit with an extended "Form C". A sizing ceiling exp(mu + 3 sigma) is then evaluated against practitioner baselines on a six-day holdout, and a decision layer prices coverage under AWS, GCP, and Azure SLA credit schedules, with the log-normal ceiling reported as the best method on coverage and net revenue.

## 2. Contributions

Ranked by significance:

- Novel: a systematic per-bin parametric-family comparison for CPU utilization on public production traces (Section 5, Tables 2-4), including tail-sensitive AD/CvM statistics. I am not aware of a prior study of this exact form; this is the paper's strongest element.
- Novel: the sigma-scaling analysis (Section 6), including the honest identification that the observed variance growth exceeds the Poisson-mechanism prediction by roughly 9x and the Form C demand-fluctuation extension. This is a real empirical finding, though its framing overstates what was confirmed (see Weakness 6).
- Reproduction: the log-normal claim itself and the mu-linear clause reproduce a 2011 industrial study; the ceiling exp(mu + k sigma) is a standard log-normal quantile and not new.
- Incremental: the SLA-pricing decision layer (Section 7) recasts a proprietary contract with public schedules; the mapping from per-bin CPU exceedance to monthly-uptime SLA tiers is the weakest technical link (Weakness 7).

## 3. Strengths

- The per-bin family comparison is large (12,479 bins across two traces), uses cluster-bootstrap CIs over VMs (abstract, Table 2), and correctly discounts the KS test as non-discriminating with fitted parameters (Section 5.2).
- The AD/CvM table (Table, label tab:advcm) is exactly the tail-focused evidence a sizing claim needs, and log-normal wins the median on all three statistics on both traces.
- Section 6 documents a sign-flip correction from an earlier draft of the sigma law and reports the units-artefact partial-Spearman control (0.82 raw dropping to 0.49 on fastStorage). Reporting that a third of the correlation was an artefact is the kind of self-check most submissions omit.
- The unified evaluation panel (Section 6/Sizing, one script, identical train/test splits and bin filters for all methods) is the right design for method comparison, and the paired per-VM sign test on SLA credits (Section 7, scoping/sla_paired.py) is the right test at the operator level.
- The reproducibility posture (Docker image, released scripts, macro-generated numbers) is above average for this venue.

## 4. Weaknesses and required revisions

1. SEVERE. The rolling-max baseline is suppressed from both results tables, and it defeats the headline claim. The text lists rolling_max as a fitted method (Section on Sizing Accuracy, bullet list) and even names it in the Section 7 interpretation ("methods that cover more often (lognorm_ctop, rolling_max) keep more revenue"), yet Table tab:sizing and Table tab:sla contain no rolling_max row. The paper's own macro file (paper_macros.tex) carries the missing numbers: on Rnd, rolling_max share-at-target is 75.68% [73.00, 78.73] versus log-normal's 73.17% [69.85, 77.24]. Rolling max wins on Rnd, and on fastStorage the two are statistically tied (82.09 vs 82.31, overlapping CIs). The abstract's claim that "the log-normal ceiling delivers the highest share-at-target coverage on both traces" is contradicted by the authors' own computed results. Fix: restore the rolling_max rows to both tables, include it in the paired sign tests, and rewrite the abstract, Section 6 conclusion, and Section 7 conclusion to state where log-normal loses or ties. A comparison table that drops the winning baseline is disqualifying if not corrected.

2. SEVERE. Broken confidence intervals for the GBM baseline. In paper_macros.tex the fastStorage GBM share is 50.81 with CI [56.70, 63.88] and the Rnd share is 44.65 with CI [45.67, 52.94]; both point estimates lie outside their own intervals. Either the point estimates and CIs come from different runs or a macro-generation bug pasted mismatched values. Every number in Tables tab:sizing and tab:sla must be regenerated in one pass from one script run, and the authors must state which run produced each cell. Until this is fixed, no quantitative claim in Sections 6-7 can be trusted.

3. SEVERE. False "non-overlapping CI" claims. Section 6 asserts the log-normal ceiling "dominates every physics-based and data-driven baseline by CI-separated margins". Against empirical_p998 on fastStorage the intervals are [79.55, 85.83] versus [74.30, 80.15]: they overlap. The Section 6 "Interpretation" paragraph repeats the claim, and the Table tab:sla caption makes a similar CI-separation claim. Fix: replace CI-eyeballing with a paired per-bin or per-VM test for every comparison, report the actual p-values, and delete every "non-overlapping" sentence that the numbers do not support.

4. SEVERE. Unexplained cohort attrition and opaque selection filters. Table 1 advertises 1,250 fastStorage VMs and roughly 500 Rnd VMs; the macros reveal 286 and 291 VMs in the fit (NVmsAll = 577), 260/258 in the scaling fit, 210/221 in the unified panel, and 106 in the "well-fitting well-proxied cohort" on which the marquee "105 of 106" direction result rests. Nowhere does the paper state the filters that discard 77% of fastStorage VMs before any distributional claim is made, nor what "well-fitting" and "well-proxied" thresholds are. Post hoc conditioning on fit quality then reporting agreement inside the surviving cohort is a textbook selection effect. Fix: a cohort-flow table (VMs in, filter applied, VMs out, exact threshold) for every analysis, plus a sensitivity analysis showing the headline shares on the unfiltered population.

5. SEVERE. Construct validity of "transaction load" on this data. The entire framing is CPU under transaction load, but Bitbrains VMs are general business-critical enterprise workloads (application servers, database servers, management machines), not transaction processors, and the load variable is network-received throughput with a median sample-level Spearman against CPU of only 0.46 (Section 6). The paper admits the proxy is weak, then builds the scaling law, the ceiling, and the revenue simulation on it. Fix: either obtain a trace with true request counts (the authors' own scoping/BORG_PLAN.md proposes one; execute it), or retitle and reframe the paper as a study of CPU utilization distributions with a load-index covariate, with the transaction mechanism clearly labelled as unverified on this data.

6. MODERATE. The scaling-law "confirmation" is weaker than the prose. The derived law (Equation eq:sigmascaling) is quantitatively wrong on this data by a factor of about nine, and the model-selection result the authors report for the rescue (Section 6, Form C paragraph) is that free Form B is AICc-preferred on 55% of VMs versus 37% for pinned Form C. A form preferred on a minority of VMs cannot "confirm the qualitative attribution ... as a fitted model". Fix: state plainly that the Poisson-arrival variance mechanism is rejected at Bitbrains's sampling resolution, present Form C as a competitive but not preferred alternative, and move the mechanism language to future work.

7. MODERATE. The SLA revenue simulation conflates constructs. AWS/GCP/Azure schedules are defined on monthly VM uptime; the paper applies them to a mean per-bin CPU-exceedance "availability" over a six-day window (Section 7), with the service-success curve Ps(C), ARPE, and the event histogram Pe(E|T) never specified for the public replication. A CPU sample above the predicted ceiling is not an availability breach. Fix: define every component of R explicitly in the paper, justify or replace the exceedance-to-downtime mapping, and label the results as a stylized cost model rather than provider-revenue estimates. Note also the text says "the four sizing methods from Section 6" while the table shows five and the method list has six; the count must be consistent everywhere.

8. MODERATE. The anchor study is unciteable and one trace row is unusable. The 2011 "industry study" being replicated has no citation, no venue, and no description beyond "proprietary"; the Origin trace occupies a row of Table 1 but appears in no analysis. A replication paper must identify what it replicates, or drop the framing. Similarly, single-archive coverage (both traces are Bitbrains, one provider, one month, 2013-era, 5-minute sampling) is thin for this journal's standards; at minimum add the Azure Public Dataset or Alibaba 2018 trace, both already cited in Section 2.

9. MODERATE. The coverage metric is near-degenerate at the stated bin sizes. Bins with as few as 12 test samples are scored on whether the ceiling covers at least 99.8% of them; with n < 500 the criterion collapses to "covers all test samples", which structurally favors the widest ceilings (and explains why rolling_max does so well). Fix: report the metric's behavior as a function of test-bin size, or use a proper scoring rule (e.g., pinball loss at the 0.998 quantile) that does not saturate.

10. MINOR. Presentation defects: the Rnd VM count renders as "~500" from a literal tilde macro in Table 1; the three-sigma one-sided coverage is 0.99865, not 0.998 (Section 3); the Zenodo DOI is promised but not given (Section 9); Figure fig:refit-ab is a PNG where the venue expects vector; the abstract reports "105 of 106" without defining the cohort; the abstract says five baselines versus four in Section 7 text versus six methods listed.

## 5. Reproducibility check

Better than average, but not yet reproducible from the paper alone. Scripts are named per claim (scoping/fit.py, scoping/unified_eval.py, scoping/refit_form_c.py, scoping/sla_paired.py) and the macro pipeline ties tables to outputs. Missing: (a) no random seeds stated anywhere in the paper for the bootstrap (B=2000), the GBM baseline, or the VM sampling ("one VM sample per trace" implies subsampling whose seed and size are unstated); (b) cohort definitions are ambiguous at every stage (Weakness 4), so a colleague could not reconstruct the 286/210/106 VM sets; (c) the GBM feature window and hyperparameters are only partially specified; (d) the EWMQ decay parameter is unspecified; (e) the Ps(C) curve and ARPE value for the revenue simulation are absent; (f) the repository URL is given but the DOI is not. Items (a), (b), and (e) block reproduction of Tables tab:sizing and tab:sla.

## 6. Comparison to prior work

Inadequate for this journal. The section cites Cortez et al., Reiss et al., Meng et al., Bodik et al., PRESS, and the trace archives, which is a reasonable start, but the SIGMETRICS/PERFORMANCE canon on workload distributions and provisioning is missing: Crovella and Bestavros on self-similarity and heavy tails; Harchol-Balter's body of work on heavy-tailed job sizes and its sizing implications; Gandhi, Harchol-Balter et al. (AutoScale, ACM TOCS 2012); Rzadca et al. (Autopilot, EuroSys 2020), which is the closest deployed system to the proposed per-bin percentile ceiling; Delimitrou and Kozyrakis (Quasar/Paragon) on interference-aware provisioning; and Kleinrock-tradition queueing baselines that predict utilization variance from arrival processes, against which the proposed mechanism should be positioned. The claim of being "unaware of a systematic per-bin log-normal test" needs an actual search: log-normality of resource usage has been examined in grid and HPC settings (e.g., Feitelson's workload modeling book and the parallel workload archive literature). Required: a substantially extended related-work section and an explicit statement of what the closest prior percentile-provisioning system does differently.

## 7. Statistical rigor

Cluster-bootstrap CIs over VMs and paired sign tests are the right instruments, and their use is a strength. However: the CI-separation claims are false in at least one named case (Weakness 3); one method's CIs exclude their own point estimates (Weakness 2); the multiple-comparison burden of five baselines times two traces times three schedules is never addressed; the selection filters that define every cohort are undisclosed (Weakness 4); and the share-at-target criterion saturates at small test-bin sizes, biasing the comparison toward wide ceilings (Weakness 9). The KS discussion is correct, and the AD/CvM addition is welcome, though without parametric-bootstrap p-values (acknowledged in Section 8) the AD/CvM medians rank families but certify nothing about absolute tail adequacy.

## 8. Writing and presentation

The prose is generally clear and the macro-driven tables are a good practice. Specific defects: abstract says five baselines, the sizing text says "four sizing methods", the bullet list has six methods, the tables show five; Section 7 text references rolling_max results that appear in no table; Table 1 promises 1,250 VMs that Section 5 quietly reduces to 286; the "~500" literal renders badly; captions for Tables tab:sizing and tab:sla assert CI conclusions the numbers do not support; the draft header still lists "Target venues: SoCC / Middleware / HotCloud / IEEE TCC" in the source comments, and the title page says "Draft". Tables are otherwise self-contained.

## 9. Fit for Performance Evaluation

The topic (parametric utilization model, fit on production traces, provisioning implication, practitioner baselines) is squarely in scope for Performance Evaluation, and the paper's structure matches the journal's accepted-paper template. The single-archive data source is below the journal's usual empirical breadth, which by itself would warrant major revision. If the authors prefer a systems venue, Middleware or ACM SoCC would tolerate the decision-layer emphasis better, but the distribution-fitting core belongs here.

## 10. Recommendation

MAJOR REVISION. The distribution-fitting contribution is real and the evaluation design is sound in outline, but the suppressed rolling-max baseline that ties or beats the headline method, internally inconsistent confidence intervals, false CI-separation claims, and undisclosed cohort filters mean the paper's central comparative claims cannot stand as written and must be recomputed and restated before the work is publishable.

## 11. Confidential comments for the editor

The macro file shipped with the source contains complete results for a rolling-max baseline that the results tables omit and that outperforms the proposed method on one of the two traces; combined with point estimates lying outside their own confidence intervals, this suggests results were assembled across runs with a strong wins-only filter, and I would ask the authors to certify a single-pass regeneration of all tables in the response letter. The underlying empirical work appears genuine and the revision path is clear, so I do not recommend rejection. Against the typical Performance Evaluation submission I review, this sits in the middle third on technical content and below median on reporting integrity in its current form; it is not submittable elsewhere as-is for the same reasons.
